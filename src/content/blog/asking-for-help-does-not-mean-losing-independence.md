## Independence can look different at every age

For some people, independence means driving to the grocery store. For others, it means choosing what to eat, managing their own schedule, or staying in a familiar home. As life changes, the way we do certain things may change too. That does not mean a person has lost their identity or their right to make choices.

## Support can make room for more choice

A little help with transportation may make it possible to keep attending a favorite class. Grocery delivery may leave more energy for cooking. Help with heavy cleaning may make it easier to enjoy the rest of the home. The National Institute on Aging describes a range of supports that can help people remain at home, from household tasks and meals to transportation and personal care.

## Make help collaborative

The best starting point is a conversation about what the person wants to keep doing. Ask, "Which tasks would you like a hand with, and which would you rather continue doing yourself?" Agree on the boundaries. A family member might handle online appointment scheduling while the older adult chooses the provider and decides what questions to ask.

## Safety and choice can work together

If falls, memory concerns, medication questions, or mobility changes are becoming an issue, talk with an appropriate health professional. A home safety review or a medication review may identify practical changes. The goal is not to remove every risk by removing every choice; it is to understand risks and make informed decisions together.

## Accepting help is a strength

Most of us rely on other people at different points in life. Asking for support can be a thoughtful way to protect energy, safety, and the activities that matter. Independence is not measured by how much a person refuses. It is also measured by whether they have the information, support, and freedom to make meaningful decisions.

## Quick takeaways

- Define independence in the older adult's own words.

- Offer help with specific tasks rather than taking over.

- Keep the person involved in decisions.

- Discuss safety concerns with qualified professionals.

Share this with someone who may need the reminder: accepting support can be one way of staying in control.

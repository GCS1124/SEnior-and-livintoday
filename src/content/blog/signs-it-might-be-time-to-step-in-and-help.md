It is not always easy to tell when an aging parent or loved one needs a little more help. They may say everything is fine, even when everyday tasks are becoming harder. And family members may hesitate to bring it up because they do not want to sound critical or make someone feel as though they are losing control of their life. But noticing changes early can give everyone more choices. The goal is not to take over. It is to understand what is becoming difficult and find support that helps an older adult remain safe, comfortable, and involved in decisions.

## The signs are often small before they become serious

You may notice that the house is not as tidy as it used to be, groceries are running low, or laundry is piling up. Maybe your parent has stopped attending a favorite activity, seems unsure about an appointment, or is having trouble keeping track of everyday responsibilities. One change does not automatically mean someone needs care. What matters is whether a pattern is developing and whether the person is having difficulty with tasks that used to feel manageable.

## Pay attention to changes in everyday routines

The National Institute on Aging recommends looking at practical areas of daily life when considering whether an older adult may need extra support. During a visit, notice whether meals are being prepared safely, personal hygiene and clothing are appropriate, the home is reasonably safe, and needed medications are available and being taken as directed. Also pay attention to falls, trouble walking, significant weight changes, confusion, loneliness, or changes in mood and memory. These observations are reasons to ask questions—not a way to diagnose someone.

## A few areas worth checking in on

**At home:** Are walkways clear? Is the bathroom comfortable to use? Is food available and is the kitchen being used safely? **Health and appointments:** Are appointments being missed? Does the person understand instructions from their health care team? **Getting around:** Have errands, stairs, or transportation become difficult? **Social connection:** Have they stopped seeing friends or taking part in activities they enjoy? **Organization:** Are bills, letters, or important paperwork becoming difficult to manage? Ask about these areas with curiosity rather than treating them like a checklist someone has failed.

## How to bring it up without hurting their feelings

A direct but caring conversation is usually better than making decisions behind someone's back. Try: "I noticed getting to the store seems harder lately. Would it help if we figured out an easier way?" Or: "You have a lot going on. Is there one thing I could take off your plate?" Avoid labels such as "You can't manage anymore" or "You need someone to look after you." Give the person time to respond. They may be worried about losing privacy, control, or the home they love.

## Start with the smallest useful change

Support can begin with grocery delivery, a weekly ride, help with heavier household tasks, a regular family check-in, or assistance organizing appointments. If health, memory, balance, or medication concerns are involved, encourage a conversation with a qualified health care professional. Some changes may have treatable causes, and a professional can help identify the right next step. If the person is open to it, ask what they want to continue doing independently and where help would make life easier.

## When a family cannot be there every day

Not every family lives nearby or has the time to provide hands-on help. With the older adult's permission, trusted relatives, friends, neighbors, or local aging-service organizations may help create a reliable support network. The Eldercare Locator can connect people with local services and Area Agencies on Aging. If there is an immediate safety or medical emergency, seek emergency assistance rather than waiting for a routine family conversation.

## Helping is not the same as taking over

An older adult should remain part of decisions about their home, routines, and support whenever possible. Ask before arranging services, explain choices, and agree on who will do what. Revisit the plan as circumstances change. A little support at the right time can help someone keep doing more of what matters to them.

## Key takeaways

- Look for patterns in meals, hygiene, mobility, appointments, medication routines, and social connection.

- Ask the older adult directly what feels difficult instead of assuming what they need.

- Start with one practical change the person agrees to try.

- Talk with a health professional about significant changes in health, memory, mood, or mobility.

- Use local aging services when family support alone is not enough.

If you have noticed changes in someone you love, start with one question: "What has become harder lately, and what would make it easier?" A respectful conversation today may help your family plan before a crisis.

The holidays bring families together. There are meals to share, stories to catch up on, photographs to take, and familiar traditions that make the season feel special. But once everyone heads home, life returns to its usual routine—and for an older adult who lives alone, that quiet can feel especially noticeable. The days after a holiday are a good time to follow up. Not because every older person is lonely or needs help, but because a visit may have brought changes to your attention that deserve a little more care.

## The visit may have shown you something important

During a holiday gathering, you may have noticed that a parent seemed more tired than usual, had difficulty moving around the home, or needed help with tasks they once handled comfortably. Perhaps they mentioned missing friends, struggling to get groceries, or feeling overwhelmed by appointments. These details can be easy to overlook when everyone is busy celebrating. A follow-up gives you a chance to understand whether it was a one-time difficulty or something that has been happening more often.

## Remember the quiet after everyone leaves

For some older adults, the transition from a busy family gathering to a quiet home can be difficult. They may miss the conversations, shared meals, and company. Social isolation and loneliness are not the same thing, and not every person who lives alone feels lonely. Still, regular social contact matters. The National Institute on Aging recommends staying connected through visits, phone calls, video chats, hobbies, classes, and community activities.

## Ask how they are doing in a real way

Instead of asking only, "Did you have a good holiday?" try a question that opens the conversation: "How have things been since everyone left?" "Have you been getting out as much as you would like?" "Is there anything around the house you could use a hand with?" Give the person time to answer. Listen for what they say—and what they may be hesitant to bring up. Do not assume that a quiet week means something is wrong, but do not dismiss a concern simply because they say they are fine.

## Check practical needs as well as feelings

Ask whether groceries, prescriptions, transportation, home heating, and upcoming appointments are taken care of. If you noticed a loose handrail, dim hallway, or difficulty using the stairs during your visit, ask whether the person would like help addressing it. If they mention a new health concern, encourage them to contact their health care professional. A family check-in is not a substitute for medical care, but it can help someone get connected to the right support.

## Make the next connection before ending the call

A vague promise to "talk soon" can easily get lost in a busy week. Suggest something specific: a Sunday phone call, lunch next week, a regular walk, or a video chat with grandchildren. If distance makes visits difficult, share responsibility among family members. One person might call, another might arrange a grocery delivery, and someone else might help with appointments—with the older adult's agreement.

## Look beyond your own family

This is also a good time to remember an older neighbor, former colleague, or friend who may not have had visitors during the holidays. A friendly call, invitation, or offer to pick up groceries can be meaningful. If someone needs help finding local services, the Eldercare Locator can connect older adults and families with community-based aging resources.

## Make checking in a habit, not a holiday task

A single call is kind. A dependable relationship is even better. Set a realistic rhythm that works for everyone and ask the older adult what kind of contact they prefer. Some people enjoy daily calls; others would rather have a weekly visit. The important thing is consistency, respect, and making sure the person knows they have someone they can reach.

## Key takeaways

- Follow up after the holiday gathering instead of waiting until the next special occasion.

- Ask about companionship, daily routines, transportation, meals, and appointments.

- Offer one specific form of help rather than a general promise.

- Create a regular check-in that fits the older adult's preferences.

- Remember older neighbors and friends who may not have a nearby support network.

After the holidays, choose one older person to check on. Make the call, ask how everyday life is going, and agree on when you will connect again. Sometimes the most meaningful part of the season is the care that continues after it.

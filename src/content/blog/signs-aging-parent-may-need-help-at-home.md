## Sometimes the changes are easy to miss

Maybe the laundry is piling up, the refrigerator is nearly empty, or your parent has stopped driving to a favorite store. Perhaps they seem more tired after errands or have started missing appointments. One change by itself may not mean anything serious. But a pattern can be a useful reason to check in and ask whether everyday tasks are becoming harder.

## Look at routines, not just the house

Notice whether your parent is keeping up with meals, bathing, dressing, housekeeping, transportation, bills, and medication routines. Ask whether they feel safe moving around the home. Pay attention to changes in balance, vision, hearing, mood, memory, or social connection. Avoid turning observations into a diagnosis. A health care professional can help evaluate changes and identify possible causes, including medication effects or treatable health issues.

## Start the conversation gently

Try saying, "I noticed errands seem to take more out of you lately. Is there anything that would make them easier?" This is more inviting than announcing that someone can no longer manage alone. Ask what matters most to your parent. They may want help with grocery delivery but prefer to continue cooking, or welcome a ride to appointments while keeping control of their schedule.

## Explore support in manageable steps

Support does not have to mean a major life change. It might begin with a weekly family visit, a transportation service, help with yard work, a home safety review, or a conversation with a local aging-services organization. The National Institute on Aging explains that support at home can include household tasks, meals, transportation, personal care, and health-related assistance. The right mix depends on the person.

## Make a plan that can change

Needs can change over time. Agree on what to try first, who will help, and when you will check in again. Keep your parent involved in decisions and revisit the plan without blame. The purpose is not to take over; it is to make daily life safer and more comfortable while preserving as much independence as possible.

## Quick takeaways

- Look for patterns across everyday activities.

- Ask open questions instead of making assumptions.

- Discuss changes with a health professional when appropriate.

- Start with small, agreed-upon supports and review them regularly.

If you are noticing changes, you do not have to solve everything in one conversation. Start by listening and choosing one practical next step together.

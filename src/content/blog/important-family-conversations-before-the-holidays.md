## A family gathering can create an opening

Holiday visits often reveal things that are easy to miss during quick phone calls. You may notice that a parent is having trouble with stairs, seems overwhelmed by errands, or is no longer enjoying an activity they once loved. Rather than turning the visit into a list of concerns, use it as an opportunity to reconnect and listen.

## Choose the right moment

Avoid raising sensitive topics in the middle of a crowded dinner or when someone is tired. Ask for a quiet conversation and explain why you want to talk. Try, "I want to make sure we understand what would make life easier for you. Could we talk about that sometime this weekend?"

## Talk about the present before the distant future

Start with everyday life: transportation, meals, home maintenance, appointments, social activities, and the tasks that feel tiring. Ask what support would be welcome. If the older adult is open to it, discuss emergency contacts, important documents, and who they would want involved if a health issue arose.

## Listen for preferences and boundaries

Family members may have different opinions, but the older adult's voice should remain central whenever possible. Do not assume that one person should take responsibility for everything. Discuss who can help with specific tasks and what professional or community resources may be useful.

## Agree on one next step

A useful conversation does not have to solve every future question. It might lead to a home safety check, a medication review with a clinician, an updated contact list, or a follow-up family call. Write down what everyone agreed to and revisit it after the holidays.

## Quick takeaways

- Choose a calm, private time to talk.

- Begin with everyday challenges and the older adult's priorities.

- Avoid making decisions for someone without involving them.

- End with one practical next step.

Before the next family gathering, think of one caring question you can ask—and make space to hear the answer.

Few conversations feel harder than asking a parent what they want at the end of their life. Many families wait for "the right time," and too often that time arrives during a hospital stay, when everyone is frightened and decisions have to be made fast.

Talking early, while everyone is calm and well, is one of the kindest things you can do for each other. Here's how to make it easier.

## Pick a calm moment, not a crisis

Choose a relaxed, private setting with time to spare: a quiet afternoon at home, a drive, or a walk. Avoid holidays packed with relatives or the days right after bad news. You're not trying to finish everything in one sitting. You're opening a door.

## Lead with love, not logistics

Start with why it matters to *you*:

- "I want to make sure I always do what you'd want. Can we talk about that?"
- "A friend just went through a hard time with her mom's care, and it made me realize I don't know what you'd want."
- "I've been working on my own plans and wondered if you've thought about yours."

Sharing your own planning takes the spotlight off them and makes it a family project rather than an interrogation.

## Listen more than you talk

Ask open questions and let silences sit. Your parent may need time, or several conversations, to get comfortable. If they shut it down, respect that and try again in a few weeks. Gentle persistence works better than pressure.

## Topics to cover over time

You don't need to cover everything at once. Over a few conversations, aim to understand:

1. **Healthcare wishes:** what matters most if they become seriously ill, and who should make medical decisions if they can't.
2. **Legal documents:** whether they have a will, power of attorney and advance directive, and where the originals are kept.
3. **Finances:** which banks, accounts, insurance policies and bills exist, and who can help manage them.
4. **Where they want to live:** staying at home, moving closer to family, or considering senior living.
5. **Final arrangements:** burial or cremation, service preferences, and any pre-arranged plans.
6. **Important contacts:** doctors, pharmacy, attorney, financial advisor, faith community and close friends.
7. **Personal wishes and legacy:** stories, family history, keepsakes and messages they want passed on.

## Write it down

Memories fade and stories change between siblings. Once your parent shares their wishes, record them in one place everyone can find. [The Living Legacy Planner](/books/living-legacy-planner/) was created for exactly this: a single, guided book for final wishes, medical details, finances, household contacts and memories.

For legal decisions such as wills, powers of attorney and advance directives, involve a licensed attorney so the documents are valid in your state.

## Include the whole family

When possible, share what you learn with siblings and other key family members so no one is surprised later. If emotions run high, a neutral third party can help keep the conversation on track.

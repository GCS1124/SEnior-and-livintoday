## There is often a caregiver behind the gathering

Someone may have planned the meal, checked on an older relative, arranged transportation, organized medications, or made sure everyone had what they needed. Caregiving can happen quietly in the background, and the person doing it may not ask for recognition.

## Appreciation should be practical

A heartfelt thank-you matters. So does offering to take a specific task. Bring a dish, handle a grocery run, sit with a loved one while the caregiver rests, or take responsibility for arranging the next appointment. Specific offers are easier to accept than "Let me know if you need anything."

## Ask what would actually help

Caregivers have different needs. One may want a few hours away; another may need help with paperwork or transportation. Ask before making plans, and respect the answer. The goal is to support the caregiver without creating more work for them.

## Remember that support can continue after Thanksgiving

A single holiday gesture is kind, but reliable help throughout the year is more valuable. Consider a recurring check-in, a shared family task calendar, or exploring local respite services. Caregivers of eligible veterans may also find resources through the VA Caregiver Support Program.

## Make room for the caregiver at the table

Invite the caregiver to enjoy the gathering rather than spending the whole day managing everyone else. Let other family members step in. Sometimes the most meaningful gift is a chance to be present as a daughter, son, spouse, sibling, or friend—not only as the person responsible for care.

## Quick takeaways

- Thank caregivers specifically and sincerely.

- Offer a task with a clear time and commitment.

- Ask what kind of help would be most useful.

- Keep supporting caregivers after the holiday.

This Thanksgiving, choose one practical way to give a caregiver time, rest, or a little breathing room.

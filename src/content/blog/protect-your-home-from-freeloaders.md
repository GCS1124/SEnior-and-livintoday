Hi, my Saltines. Let's talk about something nobody likes to talk about: people who want to move in.

Times are tough, and a lot of seniors and boomers have something other people want: a home that's paid for or a place of their own. Most people who ask for help mean well. But some see us as soft-hearted, and they think soft-hearted means easy. **We may be soft-hearted, but we are not fools.**

Here are the ground rules I'd want you to think about *before* somebody shows up with a suitcase.

## 1. Never agree to “until”

Watch out for these:

- "I just want to stay with you until I get on my feet."
- "Just until I find a job."
- "Just until I find a place."

"Until" has no end date. If your place is comfortable, there may be no hurry to find another one. Agree on a number of days, out loud, before they arrive. In many places, a guest who stays more than a few days can start to be treated like a resident, which makes asking them to leave much harder. Check the rules where you live.

## 2. Their mail does not come to your house

If they're not living there, their mail shouldn't be delivered there. They can use a friend's address or get a P.O. box. Mail at your address can look like proof that they live with you.

## 3. Set the house rules ahead of time

Most of us are pretty set in our ways, and we like our space. Say what matters to you up front: guests, quiet hours, the kitchen, the car, how long. It's much easier to set rules before they move in than after.

## 4. Renting a room? Put it in writing

If someone asks to rent a room for 30 days, that's longer than a visit. Get a basic rental agreement and fill out *everything*, even if it's your nephew. Rent, deposit, pets, emergency contact, guests, end date. Have them sign it. If you ever need to have them leave, you'll be glad you did.

## 5. Moving in with a partner? Talk first

If it's a romance, have the hard conversations before you share a roof. A lawyer can draw up what's called a **cohabitation agreement**. Think of it like a prenup without the wedding: who's bringing what, who stays if it doesn't work out, and what happens to things you buy together. Yes, it costs a fee. It's usually worth it.

## 6. Renting your own place? You're not the landlord

If you rent, you can't evict a guest yourself. Your landlord would have to do it through the court. That can strain your relationship with your landlord, so think hard before you say yes.

*This is general guidance from my experience, not legal advice. Laws are different from place to place. For contracts or anything that might end up in court, please talk to a lawyer. If you'd like to talk the situation through first, [call me](/contact/).*

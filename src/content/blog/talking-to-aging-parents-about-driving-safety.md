For many older adults, driving is about much more than getting from one place to another. It means being able to visit friends, attend appointments, shop when they want, and keep control of their day. That is why bringing up driving safety with an aging parent can be so difficult. Families may worry about a near miss, a new dent in the car, or a loved one getting lost on a familiar route. But waiting until there is a serious incident can make the conversation harder. The better approach is to start talking early, focus on safety rather than age, and work together on a plan that protects mobility and independence.

## Why this conversation deserves a thoughtful start

A driver's license can represent freedom, confidence, and identity. An older adult may hear "We need to talk about your driving" as "We are taking away your independence." That fear can make someone defensive, even when family members are genuinely concerned. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration encourages families to prepare, gather information, develop a plan, and follow through. The purpose should be to help the person stay safely mobile—not to win an argument or embarrass them.

## Notice patterns, not just one difficult drive

Possible warning signs include repeated near misses or crashes, new dents and scrapes, getting lost on familiar routes, traffic tickets or warnings, difficulty judging gaps or changing lanes, anxiety about night driving, or trouble responding to road signs and signals. Changes in vision, hearing, movement, reaction time, health, or medication can also affect driving. One sign alone does not determine whether someone is safe to drive. Driving ability should be assessed based on the person's actual skills and circumstances—not their age.

## Choose a calm moment and the right person

Avoid bringing it up during an argument, immediately after a stressful drive, or in front of a large group. A one-on-one conversation with a trusted family member may feel less threatening. Be specific about what you observed. Instead of saying, "You are too old to drive," try, "I felt worried when the car drifted into the next lane yesterday. Have you noticed anything different when you drive?" Use "I" statements, listen carefully, and give the older adult room to explain their experience.

## Ask questions that keep the conversation open

You might ask: "Are there times of day when driving feels more difficult?" "Do bright headlights or busy intersections bother you?" "Would a check-up for your vision or hearing be helpful?" "Would you be open to a professional driving assessment?" These questions make it easier to discuss practical changes without immediately demanding that the person stop driving.

## Explore ways to make driving safer

Depending on the situation, a health care professional may review health conditions and medications that could affect driving. An eye or hearing examination may be appropriate. Some drivers may benefit from a driver safety refresher course, vehicle adjustments, or a professional evaluation by a driver rehabilitation specialist. A person may also choose to limit driving to daylight, familiar routes, good weather, or less busy roads—but these changes should not be treated as a substitute for professional assessment when there are serious concerns.

## Make a transportation plan before it becomes urgent

If driving needs to be reduced or stopped, the family should already have ideas for how the person will get where they want to go. Options may include rides from family and friends, local public transportation, community or faith-based transportation programs, volunteer driver services, and ride services where suitable. Ask which activities matter most: medical appointments, grocery shopping, religious services, visiting friends, or community events. A plan should protect access to those activities, not simply remove the car keys.

## If the concern is immediate, put safety first

If someone appears unable to drive safely in the moment, do not encourage them to continue driving. Arrange a safe ride and seek appropriate professional guidance. If there is an urgent medical or public safety concern, use the relevant emergency or state reporting process. Families should check the rules and procedures in the state where the driver lives.

## Keep the older adult involved

The most constructive plan is one developed with the older adult whenever possible. Agree on what will happen next, who will help, and when the family will revisit the discussion. The conversation may take more than one sitting. That is okay. What matters is taking concerns seriously while treating the person with respect.

## Key takeaways

- Start the conversation before a serious incident occurs.

- Focus on specific driving behaviors and safety—not the person's age.

- Look for repeated warning signs and seek professional evaluation when appropriate.

- Discuss health, vision, hearing, medications, and driver assessment with qualified professionals.

- Plan transportation alternatives so the person can remain connected to the activities that matter.

If you have concerns about an older loved one's driving, do not wait for a crisis to start the conversation. Begin with care, specific observations, and a willingness to plan together. The goal is safer mobility and dignity—not simply taking away the keys.

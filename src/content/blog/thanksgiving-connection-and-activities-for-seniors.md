## The best part of the holiday may be the people

Food and traditions are important, but the heart of Thanksgiving is often the time spent together. For older adults, a busy gathering can be joyful and tiring at the same time. Thoughtful planning can make it easier for everyone to participate.

## Make the gathering comfortable

Consider seating, lighting, noise, temperature, and access to the bathroom. If someone has difficulty standing for long periods, provide a comfortable place to sit. Ask about dietary needs and offer a quieter space if the room becomes overwhelming. Small adjustments can help an older relative stay involved.

## Give everyone a way to contribute

An older adult may enjoy sharing a family recipe, choosing the music, telling a story, setting out photographs, or helping with a simple task. Do not assume that someone wants to sit out because they move more slowly. Ask what they would enjoy doing.

## Create traditions that do not depend on a big budget

A gratitude circle, family photo, short walk, board game, recipe exchange, or video call with someone who cannot travel can make the day memorable. If travel is difficult, a shorter visit or a separate meal may be more comfortable.

## Notice who may feel left out

Some older adults live alone, have lost a spouse, or cannot travel to family gatherings. A phone call, invitation, shared meal, or community event can help them feel remembered. The National Institute on Aging encourages meaningful activities and social connection as people age.

## Quick takeaways

- Plan for comfort, access, and quieter conversation.

- Invite older adults to contribute in ways they enjoy.

- Create low-cost traditions that include people who cannot travel.

- Reach out to someone who may otherwise spend the day alone.

Think beyond the menu. Choose one small way to make an older adult feel included this Thanksgiving.

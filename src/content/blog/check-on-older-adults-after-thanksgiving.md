## The quiet after a holiday can feel different

After a house full of family and conversation, an older adult who lives alone may return to a very quiet routine. That does not mean every person will feel lonely, but it is a good reminder to keep relationships active beyond special occasions.

## Make the check-in personal

Instead of sending only a quick "Hope you had a good holiday," ask about a moment they enjoyed. Did they like the meal? Was there someone they were hoping to see? Would they like to plan another visit? A specific question can open a more natural conversation.

## Look for practical needs without making assumptions

Ask whether they have groceries, transportation, or upcoming appointments covered. If they mentioned a home repair or difficulty getting out during winter weather, follow up. Offer help with one clear task rather than taking over their schedule.

## Build a regular rhythm

A weekly phone call, shared lunch, neighborhood visit, or video chat can make connection more dependable. The National Institute on Aging suggests staying in touch through in-person visits, phone calls, email, video chat, classes, hobbies, and community activities.

## Reach beyond your own family

An older neighbor, former colleague, or friend may also appreciate a call or invitation. If someone needs local aging services, the Eldercare Locator can help connect them with community resources. A small gesture can be the start of a stronger support network.

## Quick takeaways

- Follow up after the holiday, not just during it.

- Ask about both connection and practical needs.

- Offer one specific form of help.

- Make regular contact part of the routine.

Choose one older person to call this week—and make the next check-in something they can look forward to.

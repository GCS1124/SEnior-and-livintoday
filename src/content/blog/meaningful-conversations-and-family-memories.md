## A memory can open a door

Sometimes a conversation feels easier when it begins with a shared memory. "Remember when we drove to the beach?" or "What was your first job like?" can invite a story without making the moment feel like an interview. Older adults have experiences, humor, lessons, and memories that deserve room in everyday family life.

## Ask questions that invite a story

Try asking about a favorite childhood meal, a first apartment, a memorable holiday, a job they enjoyed, a song that takes them back, or a person who taught them something important. Avoid correcting small details or rushing to the next question. Listening is often more meaningful than collecting a perfect timeline.

## Use simple prompts

Look through a photo album, play a familiar song, cook a family recipe, or ask about an object that has been in the home for years. If the person enjoys it, record a story with their permission or write down a few lines afterward. Respect their choice if a memory is private or difficult.

## Make room for every kind of story

Not every memory will be cheerful. Some may bring sadness, pride, regret, or mixed feelings. Let the older adult decide how much to share. If a conversation becomes upsetting, shift gently to a topic that feels comfortable. The goal is connection, not pressure.

## Turn stories into family traditions

A monthly memory call, a shared recipe notebook, or a short recording from a grandparent can become a meaningful family tradition. These moments help younger generations understand where they come from and remind older adults that their voices matter now—not only in the past.

## Quick takeaways

- Use photos, music, recipes, or familiar objects as conversation starters.

- Ask open-ended questions and give people time to answer.

- Record or share stories only with permission.

- Let the older adult choose which memories to revisit.

This week, ask one simple question: "What is a day from your life you still remember clearly?" Then give the answer your full attention.
